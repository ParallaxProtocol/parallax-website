Parallax's consensus rules are meant to stay put. Twenty-one million coins,
proof of work, ten-minute blocks: the base layer is supposed to be slow,
boring and hard to change, and it is. So when people ask how Parallax will
ever get instant payments or private transfers, the expected answer is a
roadmap of hard forks.

There isn't one. Both are being built right now, and neither needs a single
change to consensus. This post explains how, and states plainly where each
one stands.

## Two problems every sound-money chain hits

**Ten-minute blocks are too slow to pay for coffee.** That's not a flaw to
fix; it's the price of a chain a modest computer can verify. Bitcoin's
answer was Lightning: payments that happen off-chain and settle on it.
Building that on Bitcoin took years of script workarounds and is still
waiting on opcodes, like `SIGHASH_ANYPREVOUT`, that need a soft fork.

**A public ledger is public.** Every balance and payment is visible to
anyone, forever. The usual fixes are privacy pools built on zero-knowledge
proofs, and almost all of them rest on elliptic-curve cryptography: pairing
checks, trusted setups, curve-based encryption. That's the same math a large
quantum computer is expected to break, and a public chain keeps every
ciphertext forever for whoever can break it later.

On most chains, fixing either problem means changing the protocol. On
Parallax, the full EVM sits on top of an ossified base, so both become
ordinary programs: a contract that enforces the rules, and software that
does the rest.

## Payment channels: instant, and they work offline

A payment channel is a running tab between two people, secured by a
contract. You lock LAX into the Parallax channel registry once, on-chain.
From then on, each payment is just a new version of the tab — your balance
down, theirs up — signed by both of you. Nothing touches the chain until
someone closes the tab.

Paying takes one round trip. Your wallet signs the new state and sends it;
the other side checks it and signs it back. That countersignature is both
your receipt and the thing that protects your remaining balance.

**There are no payment servers.** Signed states travel over
[Nostr](https://nostr.com), an open network of interchangeable relays that
forward encrypted messages between keys. Your wallet uses several at once.
Every payment is sealed end to end with NIP-44 encryption and NIP-59 gift
wrapping, with timestamps fuzzed, so a relay sees only that *some* envelope
went to *some* key. A relay can't forge, alter or replay anything: every
binding commitment is a signature inside the envelope. The worst a relay can
do is fail to deliver, and your wallet retransmits through the others until
the message is acknowledged.

**They even work with no internet.** The messages are small enough for QR
codes, so a payment at a market stall with no signal is three scans: the
merchant shows the invoice, you show your signed payment, they show their
countersignature. The signatures are byte-for-byte the ones the online path
uses, so the payment is just as enforceable. The device files its encrypted
backup once it's back online.

### What keeps it honest

The registry contract is the referee, and it has no owner: no admin key, no
pause switch, no upgrade proxy. A future version would be a separate
deployment, not a change to this one.

- **Cooperative closes** settle at once, on two fresh signatures over the
  final balances.
- **A one-sided close** opens a challenge window, set when the channel opens:
  from 36 blocks (about six hours) up to a month, 144 blocks (about a day) by
  default. During it, *anyone* may submit a newer state signed by both
  parties.
- **Cheating costs money.** Closing with an old state is caught by that
  challenge, and 20% of the over-claim is burned, so fraud loses money unless
  it goes unchallenged almost every time.
- **Watchtowers are trustless.** You can hand your latest states to one or
  more watchtowers to challenge on your behalf while you're offline. The
  material they hold can only do one thing — prove the truth — so the worst
  a malicious tower can do is nothing. Your own wallet is always the first
  watcher.
- **Merchants get a server-side daemon** with a REST API and signed webhooks,
  so a shop can accept channel payments without a person at the till.

Channels in this first version are direct, between two parties, rather than
routed across a network of them. That keeps the security story small enough
to state in full, and the channel docs do: every assumption and residual
risk is written down. The one that matters most is shared by every channel
design: if you're offline for the whole challenge window with no
watchtower, a cheating counterparty could close at an old state. You size
that risk with the window length and the number of towers.

## The shielded pool: private, and post-quantum

The second project is earlier: research, being designed in the open. Its
goal is a **shielded pool** for private transfers of LAX and tokens, where
nothing in the pool's security rests on elliptic curves or pairings.

The design starts from three choices.

- **STARK proofs, with no trusted setup.** Every private transfer comes with
  a STARK proving it's valid: the notes being spent exist, they haven't been
  spent before, value is conserved, and the spender holds the key. STARKs are
  built from hash functions rather than curves, and they need no setup
  ceremony whose secrets someone might have kept.
- **Spending by hash preimage, not signature.** Proving you may spend a note
  means proving, inside the STARK, that you know the secret its owner's key is
  derived from. There's no signature for a quantum computer to forge.
- **Notes encrypted with ML-KEM-768**, the post-quantum key encapsulation
  standardized by NIST as FIPS 203. Amounts, assets and recipients are
  readable only by the sender, the recipient and whoever holds a viewing key.
  That matters because harvest-now, decrypt-later is a real threat for a
  ledger that keeps every ciphertext forever.

### Why this needs no fork

An earlier post, [Covenants Without
Forks](/blog/covenants-without-forks), noted that adding post-quantum
*signatures* to an account would take a new precompile, which on Parallax is
a fork. The pool doesn't verify a new signature scheme. It verifies a STARK,
and a STARK verifier is arithmetic and hashing: the proof's commitments use
Keccak-256, which the EVM computes with a native opcode. A plain contract can
check it. The pool is a contract, a prover in the wallet, and services that
batch and relay transactions. Nothing in it asks every node to adopt new
rules.

### What it doesn't do, stated plainly

- **The boundary is classical.** LAX enters and leaves the pool from ordinary
  accounts, which sign with ECDSA. Inside the pool nothing rests on curves;
  getting in and out still does.
- **Privacy needs company.** A shielded pool hides you among its other users.
  On a young chain, that crowd starts small.
- **Proofs are big.** STARK proofs may run past 100 KB, larger than the
  transaction limits most nodes apply today. If they don't shrink enough,
  carrying them may take a change to node *policy* — what a node relays,
  never what's valid — shipped in an ordinary client release.
- **The verifier is the risk.** A pool holds funds behind custom
  cryptographic verification code, and a single missing check could create
  money from nothing. So the work runs security first, ahead of speed, cost
  and schedule. Every security property needs a specification, a test and
  an adversarial review before anything is built on top of it.

There is no date for it, and there won't be one until the cryptography and
the code have earned it.

## The pattern

Neither project needed anyone's permission. Payment channels are a contract,
a wallet and an open relay network. The shielded pool is a contract, a
prover and some services. The rules every node enforces — the supply, the
proof of work, the block interval — stay exactly where they are.

That's the bet Parallax makes: keep the base layer still, and let everything
built on it move as fast as it can be made safe.
